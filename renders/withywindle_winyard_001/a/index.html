Our story begins in a bend of the river. In 1583 a Withywindle planted sixty vines on the sunny side of the water-meadow and, having planted them, sat down. Seventeen generations have followed that example — work when the vine asks, rest when it doesn’t, and never hurry a thing that knows its own time.

Chapter One

Patience

Four hundred and forty-three harvests on the same slope. We have learned exactly two things, and one of them is when to leave the vineyard alone.

01 — The Cellar Under the Bank

The cellar was dug into the river bank long before anyone thought to write down why. It holds a steady eleven degrees all year, smells faintly of damp chalk and old oak, and asks nothing of us but that we close the door behind us. Our reds sleep there for two winters. We visit; we do not fuss.

02 — Vines Older Than the Fence

Some of the vines on the top terrace were planted by a great-great-grandmother who is remembered mostly for her temper and her plum cake. They yield very little and we would not dream of pulling them out. Old roots reach the cold gravel under the meadow; that gravel is why our whites taste of wet stone in a dry August.

03 — Nothing We Would Not Drink Ourselves

No herbicide has touched this ground in living memory. We keep bees, geese and a great deal of clover, we plough with more enthusiasm than machinery, and we ferment with what arrives on the skins. If a vintage wants to be small, we let it be small. It is a slower way to farm and a happier one, and it makes a wine that tastes like somewhere.

Husbandry

by hand

Forty-one acres between the river and the ridge — vines on the warm slope, orchard and kitchen garden behind the old wall, and a water-meadow that floods every few winters and is all the better for it. We farm organically because nobody here can think of a good reason not to.

Chapter Three

The Long Table

& second breakfast
Lunch is at one and runs until it stops

The table under the willow was made from a single elm that came down in the flood of ’71, and it has not been indoors since. We lay it whenever there are more than four of us, which is most days. There is bread from the bakehouse, cheese from up the lane, plums when there are plums, and whatever the garden feels strongly about that week.

Guests are welcome. Bring an appetite and low expectations of punctuality — and yes, second breakfast is a real booking, served at nine with a glass of Frizzante, and nobody here has ever thought that odd.

Chapter Four

Story

Seventeen generations, one river, and a family archive kept mostly in shoeboxes. What follows is the short version.

1583

A ledger in the parish chest records sixty vines set at Withy Bottom in the spring of 1583, and a dispute about a hedge. The vines survived the dispute. Since then we have made wine through three floods, two fires, one very determined aunt, and four hundred and forty-three autumns — and the hedge is still where it was.

Chapter Five

Visits

Four ways to spend a day here. All of them are led by somebody who lives on the farm, and all of them end with a glass in your hand.

Nine o’clock, in the garden

Second

Breakfast

Eggs from the geese, bread from the bakehouse, honey from the hives behind the wall, and a glass of Second Breakfast Frizzante while the mist is still in the trees. Ninety minutes. Twelve seats. Booked more often by people on their second visit than their first.

An hour along the water

Walk

the Water-meadow

The old drovers’ path follows the Withywindle from the bottom gate to the weir and back. Kingfishers if you are quiet, otters if you are lucky, and a bottle of Goldberry waiting in the shade at the turn. Bring boots between October and May; the meadow does what it likes.

Eleven degrees, all year

Cellar

Hours

Down eleven worn steps into the bank, where the reds sleep in oak that is older than most of the family photographs. We taste straight from the barrel, in the dark, with a lantern — not for atmosphere, but because there has never been a good reason to run a cable down there.

Three weeks in September

Harvest

by Hand

Everything here is picked by hand, into small crates, by whoever turns up. Work a morning on the rows and you eat at the long table with the rest of us — which is not a reward so much as the arrangement. Sign the harvest book at the gate; we will send word when the fruit is ready.

Cellar Door

The door is the low one under the ivy, and it sticks. Tastings run twice a day and the long table is laid at one; both are best booked, though we have never actually turned anyone away. Wines can be collected here, sent by the case, or drunk on the spot — which we recommend.
